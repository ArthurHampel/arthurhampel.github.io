Genius gatherings

Every so often, an improbable number of extraordinary people were together at once: the same city, the same clubs, the same rooms. It happens too often to be luck.

Why there? Why then?

Athens, 5th century BC

In roughly one century, one city-state produced the founders of philosophy, drama, history, and democratic politics. Source: Fifth-century Athens.

Pericles Socrates Plato Aeschylus Sophocles Euripides Aristophanes Herodotus Thucydides Phidias

Florence, 1504

Leonardo and Michelangelo were commissioned to paint battle scenes on opposite walls of the same council hall in the Palazzo Vecchio, while Machiavelli served in the same government and the young Raphael traveled to Florence to study them both. Source: The Battle of Anghiari.

Leonardo da Vinci Michelangelo Machiavelli Raphael

Birmingham, 1765–1791

The Lunar Society met monthly on the Monday nearest the full moon, for light to ride home by: a dinner club that helped drive the Industrial Revolution. Source: The Lunar Men.

Erasmus Darwin Matthew Boulton James Watt Joseph Priestley Josiah Wedgwood

Edinburgh, 1750–1790

The founders of modern economics, geology, chemistry, and sociology all worked in one city of about fifty thousand, many in the same clubs. A visitor, the King's chemist, remarked: "Here I stand at what is called the Cross of Edinburgh, and can, in a few minutes, take fifty men of genius and learning by the hand." Source: Scottish Enlightenment.

David Hume Adam Smith James Hutton Joseph Black Adam Ferguson

Philadelphia, 1776 & 1787

Thirteen colonies of about 2.5 million people put the founders into the same rooms, one statehouse producing the Declaration of Independence in 1776 and the Constitution eleven years later. Source: Founding Brothers.

Benjamin Franklin George Washington Thomas Jefferson John Adams James Madison Alexander Hamilton

Brussels, 1927

The fifth Solvay Conference gathered 29 physicists to debate the newly formulated quantum theory. Seventeen of the 29 attendees were or became Nobel laureates. Source: Solvay Conference.

Albert Einstein Niels Bohr Marie Curie Max Planck Paul Dirac Werner Heisenberg Wolfgang Pauli Erwin Schrödinger Louis de Broglie

Bell Labs, 1947–1969

One industrial laboratory produced the transistor (1947), information theory (1948), the solar cell (1954), and foundational work on the laser, and later Unix and the C programming language. Ten Nobel Prizes and five Turing Awards have been awarded for work completed there. Source: Bell Labs.

Claude Shannon William Shockley John Bardeen Walter Brattain Ken Thompson Dennis Ritchie

Silicon Valley, 1957–2010s

In 1957, eight young scientists, the "traitorous eight," quit William Shockley's laboratory to found Fairchild Semiconductor; their alumni founded Intel, AMD, and Kleiner Perkins. In 1975–76 the Homebrew Computer Club produced the Apple I. In 1998, two Stanford PhD students founded Google in a Menlo Park garage. In the 2000s, the "PayPal mafia" went on to found or fund Tesla, SpaceX, LinkedIn, YouTube, Yelp, and Palantir: one valley, five overlapping generations of builders. Sources: Traitorous eight, Homebrew Computer Club, PayPal Mafia.

Robert Noyce Gordon Moore Steve Jobs Steve Wozniak Larry Page Sergey Brin Peter Thiel Elon Musk
